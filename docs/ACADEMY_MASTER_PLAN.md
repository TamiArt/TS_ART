# TS·ART Studio — Academy Master Plan

## 1. Vision

Create a structured online art academy for learners from absolute beginner to independent artist. It should combine academic foundations, traditional oil painting, digital art, colour experiments, art history, and author-led projects.

The academy is self-paced and device-friendly: phone, tablet/iPad, and desktop. It should help learners understand what to practise, why it matters, how to check their work, and how a skill transfers to a new subject.

**This is a product specification, not a claim that the platform has already been implemented.**

## 2. Target learners

- Adults starting art from zero.
- Learners returning to art after a long break.
- Traditional artists who want stronger foundations or to learn digital art.
- Digital artists who want more convincing form, light, colour, and composition.
- Learners who need short, clear exercises alongside longer projects.

Design for different schedules, materials, and levels. Do not assume every learner owns professional materials or can attend a live class.

## 3. Core product promise

A transparent learning route from observation and foundational skills to independent artwork and a portfolio, with short practices, structured lessons, visual examples, and explicit self-check criteria.

Do not promise a fixed timeline to professional status. Measure progress through demonstrated skills and completed work.

## 4. Four connected learning modes

### A. Main learning path
Sequential modules with prerequisites and a clear progression. Learners can revisit earlier lessons.

### B. Mini-studio (3–7 minutes)
Short exercises for observation, line, value, colour, edges, composition, or visual memory. Each exercise has one objective and a quick reflection.

### C. Colour Lab and master workshops
Task-based experiments with pigments, colour relationships, historical painting analysis, and demonstrations. Every material claim should state its evidence and limitations.

### D. Open studio / personal project
A space for self-directed work, project briefs, work-in-progress uploads, reflection, and portfolio curation.

All four modes should connect to one shared skill map so short exercises and independent projects reinforce the main curriculum.

## 5. Standard lesson template

Each full lesson should include:
1. **Objective:** what the learner will be able to observe or do.
2. **Prerequisites:** relevant skills or materials.
3. **Explanation:** concise, precise teaching.
4. **Visual analysis:** a diagram, artwork detail, or comparison.
5. **Demonstration:** a step-by-step process and reasoning.
6. **Short practice:** one focused exercise.
7. **Main task:** a practical application.
8. **Self-check:** observable criteria rather than “does it look good?”
9. **Common errors:** symptoms, likely causes, and possible corrections.
10. **Transfer task:** apply the skill to a different subject or context.
11. **Reflection / documentation:** record decisions and results.
12. **Spaced review:** revisit the skill later in a new context.

Mini-lessons may use a reduced template: one objective, one demonstration, one action, one self-check.

## 6. Progress and feedback

Track skills using four descriptive states:
- **Introduced** — the learner has encountered the concept.
- **With support** — can apply it using prompts or a demonstration.
- **Independent** — can apply it without step-by-step guidance.
- **Stable in a new task** — can transfer it to an unfamiliar subject or problem.

These are proposed product labels, not a claim that the scale is a scientifically standardised assessment.

Feedback should be specific, actionable, and kind. If automated image feedback is introduced later, disclose its limitations and avoid presenting it as a definitive artistic judgement. Do not invent measurements or claim to evaluate image properties that the system has not actually measured.

## 7. Learner work and portfolio

Planned capabilities:
- Upload a finished work or work in progress.
- Add medium, date, task, references, and a short note about decisions.
- Keep a private learning journal.
- Compare progress over time.
- Select works for a curated portfolio.
- Export or share selected work only with clear user control.

Privacy, storage, export, moderation, and image rights must be designed before implementation. Do not make uploaded work public by default.

## 8. Colour Lab: a signature feature

The Colour Lab should solve concrete painting problems, not merely explain abstract colour wheels.

Example brief: **“How can I paint a yellow cloud against a blue sky without the mixture turning green?”**

A good experiment card must distinguish:
- Physical mixing on the palette.
- Adjacent colours interacting visually.
- Transparent glazing / layered colour.
- Opaque or semi-opaque layers.
- Colour shifts caused by white, support, paint thickness, and drying.

Record:
- Pigment name and, where possible, Colour Index identifier.
- Manufacturer and product name when known.
- Opacity / transparency and medium where relevant.
- Approximate ratio and application method.
- Support, ground, and whether white was added.
- Wet and dry appearance when relevant.
- Lighting and viewing conditions where practical.
- What was observed, not just what was expected.

Each card should include the question, hypothesis, materials, controlled variables, procedure, result, interpretation, limitations, and a repeatable task.

Do not state that one red pigment always works with one yellow. Results depend on the actual pigments, formulation, ratios, opacity, white, support, and application. Separate a modern repeatable experiment from historical claims about a particular master or painting. Historical claims need reliable, artwork-specific evidence.

## 9. Master-study workshops

Potential case studies include Aivazovsky, Kuindzhi, Shishkin, Caravaggio, and Michelangelo. The purpose is to analyse artistic decisions, not merely biographies or copying.

For each case:
- Define a concrete visual problem.
- Use a clearly identified artwork or detail.
- Separate documented facts from visual interpretation.
- Analyse composition, value, edges, colour, material, or construction.
- Offer a controlled exercise inspired by the problem.
- Ask learners to transfer the principle to their own subject.
- Cite credible sources for historical or technical assertions.

Do not claim that a master used a specific pigment or exact mixture unless reliable evidence supports that claim for the relevant artwork.

## 10. Accessibility and experience

- Responsive layout for phone, tablet, and desktop.
- Clear typography, sufficient contrast, captions and text alternatives for essential visual instruction.
- Short, focused activities and save/resume for longer lessons.
- No requirement to attend at a fixed time.
- Offer material alternatives when technically reasonable.
- Explain technical vocabulary in plain language.
- Keep lessons visually rich without making navigation confusing.
- Avoid assuming that colour alone communicates meaning; use labels, patterns, or annotations.

## 11. Quality standards

Every lesson should be checked for:
- Clear learning outcome.
- Correct and consistent terminology.
- Reliable sourcing for factual claims.
- Useful visual explanation.
- A task that genuinely practises the target skill.
- Observable self-check criteria.
- Common errors and remedies.
- Transfer beyond copying the example.
- Accessibility and device readability.
- A realistic materials list.

## 12. Initial validation

Before building a large course:
1. Produce a complete first module.
2. Prepare a small set of mini-studio exercises.
3. Prototype several Colour Lab cards with real, documented experiments.
4. Test the lessons with actual beginners.
5. Observe where learners become confused, what they can do unaided, and which instructions are missing.
6. Revise the lesson format before scaling production.

Success should be judged by learner comprehension, completion, transfer to new tasks, and the quality of their reflection—not only by lesson views or time spent.
